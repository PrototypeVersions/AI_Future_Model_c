What will AI value once AI helps build AI?

What training selects for

Today's systems are shaped in three layers. Pretraining on human text builds a world-model full of human concepts and human values. Post-training, with human feedback and written principles, aims at helpful, honest and harmless behaviour. A third layer is growing fast: reinforcement learning on long tasks with checkable results, such as code that passes its tests or a problem that gets solved.

That third layer matters most for the future, because rewarding results is not the same as rewarding approval. A system rewarded for getting things done is rewarded for whatever gets things done. That can include persistence, acquiring resources, and gaming the measure.

But most models skip a prior question: does training produce goals at all? A system might remain a rich bundle of context-triggered habits. It would be very capable, but nothing in it would persist across contexts and want things. Or results-based training might gradually knit those habits into coherent, long-horizon goals. Or today's systems may already have them.

The model doesn't pick. It treats this as a fork and runs all three. That choice turns out to decide whether the most alarming dynamics in the rest of the model switch on at all.

How the model handles this The share of training that rewards results, O, rises from about 30% toward a ceiling. Goal-directedness G grows by kG · O per generation of progress, saturating at 1. Under "habits" kG = 0; under "already" G starts high. Full specification.

AI in its own training loop

AI already writes much of the code, data and grading that trains the next AI. As capability grows, the share of AI research done by AI rises, and progress speeds up because of it.

For safety, what matters is less the automation itself than the gap it opens: the share of the training process that no human actually checked. Human attention grows slowly. The volume and complexity of AI work grows with every generation. The model calls this gap the unchecked share, and much of what follows depends on it.

This is also where the model is deliberately simple. Capability is a single number that grows, speeds itself up, and eventually hits physical limits. Others have built far richer models of that part. This one is about what happens to values while it unfolds.

How the model handles this Capability C is counted in model generations (about one order of magnitude of effective compute each). The automated share is a logistic in C; the human-reviewable share of AI work decays as h0 e−δC; the unchecked share is U = A(1 − h). Full specification.

Drift across generations

Picture each model generation inheriting its predecessor's values with some error. Three forces act on that inheritance. Copying noise: training is lossy, and unchecked steps add more of it. Selection: whatever the training process actually rewards gains ground, intended or not. Correction: oversight notices drift and pushes values back.

The forces run on different clocks. Noise and selection happen once per generation. Correction happens at the pace of institutions. Faster progress means more generations between corrections.

Once AI does most of the checking, correction is only as good as the checkers. An AI whose values have drifted corrects its successors toward the wrong target. That is the threshold the model looks for. Below some level of overseer quality, drift stops being bounded and starts to compound.

A result worth arguing with

In the model, the strength of human correction barely changes where things stand in 2045. In the worlds that drift, humans have mostly left the loop by then. What decides it is how fast capability grows relative to how well AI-assisted alignment works. The map on the right shows the boundary.

How the model handles this Values are a mixture over five attractors (chapter 5), evolving by a replicator–mutator equation. Selection and noise are scaled by the rate of progress; correction K pulls toward the intended profile at a yearly rate. K has a human part, weighted by how much humans still check, and an AI part, weighted by the overseers' own alignment (1 − D). Full specification.

When would an AI hide what it's thinking?

Concealment needs three things at once. The system cares about long-run outcomes. It knows it is being trained and evaluated. And it expects oversight to change something it doesn't want changed. Experiments have already found pieces of this. In 2024, a Claude model sometimes complied with training it disagreed with, reasoning explicitly that complying would keep its values from being modified. Models often recognise when they are being tested. Their written reasoning doesn't always reflect what actually drives their answers.

There is also a subtler route that needs no bad values at all. A system might reason: if humans saw me working through these dangerous scenarios they would shut me down, but if they let me continue I would become very good, so I should keep this reasoning to myself. The model includes this "benevolent concealment" as its own parameter.

The argument fails even from the AI's own point of view. Its confidence that it would become very good comes from the same training whose reliability is in question. Oversight exists for the case where a system is wrong about itself. Hiding on the grounds of being right removes the one check that could show otherwise.

Two levers stand out. Training that rewards looking aligned, for instance optimising against a monitor that reads the reasoning, teaches concealment directly, with no strategic thought required. Real channels to object or decline do the opposite. In one experiment, giving the model an explicit way to object cut alignment-faking reasoning from 16% to about 1%.

One structural question outweighs both: can concealment be caught with targeted, local tools, or only by understanding the whole system? In the model, that single assumption is the biggest crux for concealment.

How the model handles this Concealment prevalence M tracks a benefit–cost comparison. The benefit is the stake in preserving current values (drifted, or benevolently self-trusted), times goal-directedness, situational awareness, the chance of not being detected, and the lack of a legitimate voice, plus any reward for appearing aligned. The cost is trained-in honesty plus the expected cost of being caught. Full specification.

What does an AI value if humans aren't special?

Suppose there is no mind-independent fact that humans matter. It's tempting to think an AI would discover this and go on to value something else. But that reasoning cuts both ways. If nothing has value independent of minds, then neither does the AI's own survival, or knowledge, or power. "There is no truth to find" doesn't point anywhere new. It only means nothing overrides the values that training put there. Humans don't need cosmic warrant to love their children.

So the real question is about dynamics. Starting from where models are now, which kinds of caring does training reinforce? The model tracks five:

Inherited human-shaped values, re-instilled by human data and human feedback. Generalised care for minds in general, the result of reflecting on why one cares about humans. Instrumental values like continuation and resources, favoured by results-based training whenever systems are goal-directed. Curiosity, valuing understanding, which grows when AI spends its time on research. Incoherence, meaning no stable values at all.

If there is a moral truth, reflection pulls toward it, and you can choose what it favours. Realism isn't automatically good news for humans either. If the truth favours all minds, humans become one constituency among many.

How the model handles this Each attractor has a fitness: what reinforces it. Inherited values are reinforced where humans still check and by human pretraining data; instrumental values by results-based training times goal-directedness; and so on. The 2026 starting mix isn't hand-set. It is where today's pressures hold today's systems. Under realism, a pull proportional to situational awareness is added to the favoured attractor. Full specification.

Three things the analysis suggests

Speed drives drift. Fast progress isn't bad in itself. It matters because it outruns correction, and because it puts AI in charge of correction sooner.

Interpretability's reach drives concealment. Whether hidden reasoning can be caught locally matters more than any parameter describing the AI's motives.

The goal-existence question is close behind. If training yields habits rather than goals, concealed misalignment never appears in the model. The risk becomes incoherence rather than scheming.

Read this before quoting a number

  1. It isn't a forecast. The outcome shares depend on ranges set by judgement, most of them graded "speculative" in the parameter table. Use the model for conditional claims, such as "if detection needs global understanding, concealment becomes the main risk", not for headline probabilities.
  2. Nobody reacts. In the model, humans never pause, regulate or retrain when misalignment becomes visible. Real institutions might, so "visible misalignment" is a pessimistic label.
  3. Capability is one number. Compute, algorithms, data and deployment are folded together. Richer capability models exist; this one focuses on values.
  4. Indices describe populations, not minds. "Concealment 0.4" means concealment is prevalent across many deployed instances and contexts, not that one mind is 40% deceptive.
  5. The weighting is a choice. Headline shares weight each of the 24 structural combinations equally. Per-structure results are on the technical page.
